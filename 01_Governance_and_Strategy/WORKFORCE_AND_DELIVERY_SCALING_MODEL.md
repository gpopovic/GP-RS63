# Workforce and Delivery Scaling Model

## Purpose

Build delivery capacity economically and safely: validate capability through bounded work before committing enduring Core-Team capacity, then scale proven units without sacrificing knowledge, quality, compliance, or customer continuity.

## Engagement Portfolio

| Engagement mode | Use when | Control mechanism | Exit or promotion signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time-bounded specialist engagement | Capability, demand, or fit is uncertain | Explicit scope, duration, access limit, confidentiality, substitution and offboarding plan | Evidence of repeatable contribution or defined end of need |
| Outcome-based work package | Deliverable and acceptance evidence can be defined | Versioned work package, acceptance criteria, quality gate, change control, payment or acceptance evidence | Accepted result plus sustainable quality and handover evidence |
| Managed delivery cell | Several contributors deliver a coherent outcome repeatedly | Named accountable lead and trained deputy or equivalent continuity route, shared definition of done, capacity plan, integration cadence, continuity coverage | Stable flow, quality, economics, collaboration, and customer evidence |
| Core Unit | A capability is strategic, recurring, trusted, and economically justified | Durable capability ownership, knowledge base, succession plan, quality and security controls | Expand, split, redesign, or retire based on portfolio evidence |
| Scaled branch | A Core Unit has a repeatable operating model and demand beyond its safe span of control | Branch charter, independent delivery and quality capacity, interfaces, transition plan, and governance link | Standalone performance and controlled integration evidence |

Engagement type is an operating decision, not a substitute for the legally correct employment, contractor, supplier, tax, data-access, and country arrangement.

## Core-Unit Admission

A delivery cell may be proposed as a Core Unit only when evidence shows:

- repeatable accepted outcomes across representative work, not one successful assignment;
- quality, security, traceability, and handover discipline at the required level;
- positive unit economics after realistic coordination, replacement, tool, quality, and risk costs;
- reliable collaboration, knowledge sharing, and a viable continuity or substitution route;
- strategic relevance or recurring demand that justifies durable company attention; and
- no unresolved material workforce, legal, privacy, supplier, or customer-access constraint.

The Cross-Border Delivery Manager, Finance and Risk Controller, Quality Manager, Workforce and Operational Effectiveness Analyst, Employment and Workforce Compliance Officer, and relevant Product Owner prepare the evidence. Company leadership decides the Core-Unit change.

## Cross-Border Focus and Continuity

Each recurring cross-border delivery cell records a working agreement with its named lead and deputy or equivalent continuity route, overlapping decision window, asynchronous hand-off convention, escalation channel, expected response classes, and protected focus periods. The agreement protects the delivery team from unbounded customer interruption without insulating it from relevant product context, customer outcomes, or evidence-based challenge. No party promises 24/7 availability by default; incident or support commitments must be explicit, staffed, and priced.

For the initial Germany–India delivery pattern, use a three-hour shared core-contact window of `09:00–12:00 Europe/Berlin`. This is `12:30–15:30 Asia/Kolkata` during Central European Summer Time and `13:30–16:30 Asia/Kolkata` during Central European Time. The named cell leads confirm the actual working agreement for each team and customer; this default is not an availability promise.

## Branch or Split Readiness

A Core Unit may be split only when its work interfaces are sufficiently stable, its knowledge is documented, quality and security ownership can be replicated, and each branch has a named accountable lead, capacity plan, decision rights, integration protocol, and customer-continuity plan. Splitting for headcount alone is not a reason.

## Guardrails and Downsides

| Risk | Required countermeasure |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge loss or contractor churn | Handover acceptance, versioned documentation, pairing, minimum viable substitution and offboarding evidence |
| Work-package incentives that trade quality for speed | Acceptance based on quality evidence, not delivery volume alone; retain independent validation and defect remediation duties |
| Fragmented ownership and expensive coordination | One accountable outcome owner, explicit interfaces, dependency ageing, and measured coordination cost |
| Misclassification, local labour, tax, or contractor risk | Employment and Workforce Compliance Officer and General Counsel review the actual arrangement, not only its label |
| Overuse of temporary labour for strategic capability | Core-Unit admission review based on strategic relevance, repeatability, and total cost of risk |
| Unfair or noisy performance decisions | Comparable work classes, confidence and context indicators, intervention before escalation, and integrity/privacy controls |
| Branches that diverge from Triton's standards | Shared configuration baseline, quality/security gates, KPI contract, and periodic integration review |

## KPIs

Use balanced measures: accepted-outcome rate, quality-adjusted cycle time, rework, handover completeness, replacement readiness, cost of coordination, unit contribution after risk cost, dependency age, customer outcome, retention risk, and Core/branch transition success. Do not use raw hours, ticket count, output volume, or online presence as standalone performance measures.
